I recently completed a school animation project. Although this was not my first experience working in a group, it was the first time I had completed a full 3D animated short film as part of a team. The group itself was fairly large, and the project lasted from last year's R&D stage into this year, a much longer period than I initially expected. At first, I believed we had more than enough time, but I was completely wrong.

The Importance of Visual Consistency

In collaborative animation production, maintaining a unified visual style is often considered essential to the integrity of the project. However, during the actual production process, individual artistic expression and different creative habits can easily disrupt that sense of consistency. As the director, one of the first mistakes I made was failing to ensure that every team member clearly understood the visual direction we were aiming for. For animated works, visual consistency not only helps maintain audience immersion, but also supports asset integration and shot continuity during post-production. Even after we recognised and adjusted this issue, the production process was still far from smooth.

As the project progressed, I began to realise that everyone had slightly different ideas about what “visual consistency” meant. Some members believed the visual design should reflect the worldbuilding and narrative background more strongly, while others preferred a simpler style in order to save production time. There were also differences in how people interpreted the core themes of the story, which led to completely different visual preferences. We all expressed our own opinions, and this was not entirely a negative thing — in some ways, it expanded the creative possibilities of the project. However, from a production standpoint, these discussions and revisions significantly slowed down our workflow.

In order to maintain overall consistency, I eventually had to make the final decisions myself, which inevitably limited some members' space for personal expression. Yet even when the team shared the same visual development references, individual traces still appeared through shape language, animation timing, and colour preferences. These differences became especially noticeable during the later stages of production, when all assets were finally placed within the same cinematic language.

Reflection

Through this project, I gradually realised that unity within collaborative work does not mean completely erasing individual style. Instead, it is more like a continuous process of negotiation and balance. Perhaps what truly matters is not making everyone identical, but finding a way for different forms of expression to coexist within a shared creative framework.
